Preface

In 1969 Herbert A. Simon wrote The Sciences of the Artificial — the study of artifacts: things made by art, standing between an inner environment (their own substance and organization) and an outer environment (the surroundings in which they work). Simon gave the sciences their language. This guide is their practice, learned from twenty years of one practitioner's builds — from a coin counter in 2005 to a signal-acquisition system in 2026.

Everything in this book is one of three things, and it says which: a trace from that record — a build, a number, a sentence the practitioner actually said; Simon's frame, named as his; or the guide's synthesis, labeled as such. Where the record is silent, the book is silent.

A science tells you what is true. A practice tells your hands what to do. This book is for the hands.

How to use this guide. Read in order, or jump — the chapters stand alone and the nav above follows you. Each part ends with a REP: a small exercise you actually perform. The writing boxes keep what you write in this browser only. Two instruments are live inside the book: the crossing in Part II and the triangulation matrix in Part IV. Run them. A guide you only read is a science; a guide you run is a practice.
Part I

The Two Environments

Simon's ground — every artifact stands between two worlds.

An artifact is a meeting point. Inside it: an inner environment — its own substance and organization, the way it is put together. Outside it: an outer environment — the surroundings in which it must work, with goals of their own. The artifact succeeds when the inner environment is adapted to the outer environment's goals — without either side pretending to be the other. The inner does not become the weather; the outer does not become the machine. They meet, and the meeting is the artifact.

One consequence governs everything after this chapter: neither side sees the other's interior. Each side knows only its own side; the other exists through its responses. You never hold the other environment in your hand — you hold what crosses back. Design for the crossing, not for an interior you will never see.

The First Principles, as the practitioner holds them

1

Environment

What does not move. The ground both sides stand on — the part of the situation you do not get to renegotiate mid-build. Name it first; everything else is built in relation to it.

2

Fields of Interaction

What holds across a gap without touching it. Two environments that never touch still act on each other — through a field, through an interface, through responses. The gap is not empty; it is where the work happens.

First Principles 1–2 and the definitions in Part II are the practitioner's own formulations, kept from his earlier reading of this material and marked there as interpretation. Simon's frame — inner environment, outer environment, adaptation — is the ground they stand on.

An artifact is a meeting point — an interface — between an inner environment and an outer environment.

Simon's frame · The Sciences of the Artificial (1969)

Part II

The Layer

The discovery, reconstructed — the crossing everyone looks past.

Between the two environments there is a layer everyone looks past: the crossing itself. Twenty years of one builder's work — payments, search, type systems, sacred text, chat rooms, signal hardware — looks like twelve unrelated territories. Underneath, one circuit runs in all of them, unchanged:

SIDE · INTERFACE · GENERATOR · GATE · ARTIFACT

Something stands on one side. It crosses an interface — and only meaning travels: the interface is the cost of carrying across, plus the component that transcribes meaning from one side's terms into the other's. A generator produces the instances — never hand-made one by one. A gate judges: a test, a checklist, a human verdict. What passes stands as an artifact. What fails returns — as information, along the return path, to the side it came from.

Part III

The Generator

The practice itself — build the grammar, not the sentences.

The practitioner's whole method compresses into one line, in his own words:

“Grammar -> I build grammar.”

The practitioner · verbatim, from the record

For every domain he enters, he builds the finite rule-set that generates its valid instances — Pāṇini's shape, the pattern of the Aṣṭādhyāyī: not the sentences, never the sentences — the grammar that makes them. A payment grammar, a grammar of test cases, of configs, of a type system, of collaboration itself. The instances are then cheap, because the expensive thing — the rule — was built once.

Corollaries, from the record

  • Never release code; release the generator. Asked for a “Python Hello World,” his answer (the guide's synthesis of his standing reply) is a playground: a dropdown, a text area, a play button — an instrument the other person runs, not a program they merely read.
  • You don't practice the 10,000 things; you practice the one generator. Outputs are cache. When the UBS KYC estate needed 10,000 test cases, the hand-made cases were the side — the generator was the work, and coverage went from 65% to 99%.
  • Run first, then setup — bottom up, three levels max. You must be able to steer it, run it, hold it, before you may configure it. Setup serves the run, never the reverse; a setup deeper than three levels is a sign the run was never held.

“Always think simple. If the solution is complex, the problem is wrong. Don't attempt.”
— THE SIMPLE LAW · THE PRACTITIONER, VERBATIM

The shipping bar

Solve it once — for yourself, the judge. Then solve it again — as demonstration, for the acceptor. In his parachute form: someone has to try it before putting others in the parachute. The pilot rides his own stack first; the report follows the run. Past solvings bank nothing — can I solve it now is the question, because the generator must run in the present tense.

And when words fail — when testimony is discounted on arrival, as it reliably is — demonstration is the bedrock. Don't argue the grammar. Run it.

Rep 3 · Do it now

Write a generator, not an instance

Name one thing you make repeatedly by hand — a report, a checklist, a message, a test, a meal plan. Now write the generator for it: the finite rules such that any valid instance falls out of them. Rules, not the next instance. If your answer is a single example, start over — you wrote a sentence, not a grammar.

Part IV

The Gate

Judgment — nothing stands until it passes a gate it did not write.

Every crossing ends at a gate. The rule is absolute: nothing stands until it passes a gate it did not write. The producer of a thing is disqualified from being its judge — not from distrust, but by construction: an updater grading its own output cannot see its own drift, so drift becomes invisible.

The triad

Operator 1The one who runs the crossing. Singular — one pair of hands on the instrument.
Acceptor N — boundedThe judges who say what “good enough” means. Finite, named, and not the operator.
Reality M — unboundedThe final judge, which accepts no arguments: contact with the real world, where the artifact either works or doesn't.
Remove the acceptor……and drift becomes invisible by construction. The silent failure is not error; it is error with no witness.

The constitution, signed before the work

Before a crossing runs, its gate is fixed in a constitution: C = { Intent, Hypothesis, Konditions, Scenarios }. The hypothesis is the acceptance — and it is held blind from the producer (criterion opacity), so the work cannot quietly bend itself toward the test it can see.

Mount is practice: intent becomes membrane, the artifact is generated under it, still held. Unmount is performance: the artifact goes live only when the gate passes. On failure — revert to the last good artifact and raise new intent. Never patch. A patch is a crossing that skipped its gate.

The triangulation matrix — only for the uncertain

The certain needs no matrix: it is the checklist, and it just runs. The matrix is reserved for the genuinely uncertain, and it works like this. A question is put to two sides. Each side answers yes or no, with a confidence on one scale: −1 is “I don't know” — the floor, not a “no” — and +1 is full confidence. Where the two sides agree and their shared confidence passes a set threshold, the answer becomes canon. Canon is not forever: it carries a shelf life of six months, then it is re-checked. No privileged frame — each side knows only its own side; the other exists through its responses.

The stop law

Know the stop condition before starting. Figure out Y — the exit — then fill X. The moment X becomes some Y you never figured out, leave.
— THE STOP LAW · FROM THE RECORD

Judgment runs in three nested loops: the inner loop, where the work is checked as it is made; the outer loop, the human test; and the outermost loop — contact with reality itself, the public launch. Time is the only cost. Reality is the ultimate judge. Every gate before the outermost is a rehearsal for it.

Part V

The Instruments at Hand

The layer, within reach — where the practitioner runs this today.

The guide closes not with a theory but with the surfaces on the practitioner's own desk — each one the circuit, wearing work clothes.

The pi0 passthrough

A 240×135 display on a separate small computer, reached through one port: display out, gamepad in. The kernel asks one question at a time on its corners, and the human answers by button. One port, one question, one answer — the interface kept so small that only meaning can fit through it. That smallness is not a limitation; it is the discipline of Part II, enforced by hardware.

The busy board

A box of real controls — knobs, buttons, switches — whose job is to keep the hands practicing: “keep yourself busy.” Its controls carry no labels to memorize; their only signal is change. A practicing instrument, not a performing one: mount is practice, and the board is where practice lives between performances.

The store

Everything has an address; the record is signed. What crosses between sessions is the transcription — canonical, checked, compressed — never the raw original. Memory, in this practice, is not a warehouse of everything that happened; it is the set of addresses and jumps worth spending on, and forgetting is handled by holding both transcriptions side by side and reading the diff.

And this guide

This page is itself an artifact standing between an inner environment and an outer one — and you have been practicing on it since Part I. Its writing boxes are a store with addresses. Its circuit is a generator you ran. Its matrix is a gate you stood at. The layer was under your hands the whole time; that is the only way it is ever learned.
