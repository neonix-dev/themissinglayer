The Missing Layer

A Practitioner’s Guide to the Sciences of the Artificial

When I’m in the middle of a problem, surrounded by systems that already exist, what is the least I need to do to get it resolved?

What I cannot create, I do not understand. Richard Feynman. If a mind like that can write it and leave it behind, I will take it as the bar.

Where the words come from

An artifact is where two sides meet

Herbert A. Simon, in The Sciences of the Artificial (1969), describes an artifact as a meeting point: an inner environment, the stuff and organization of the thing itself, and an outer environment, the surroundings it works in. Natural science asks how things are. The sciences of the artificial ask how things ought to be, to reach a goal. The vocabulary is Simon’s. The practice is this guide’s.

First principles

Six words, in the order a reader needs them

  1. EnvironmentWhat doesn’t move. What everything is measured against and no one stands on.
  2. Fields of interactionWhat holds across a gap without touching. Present only while something sustains it: iron by memory, a wire by current. Seen only by what moves toward it. One field, one interface.
  3. Artificial systemAnything with an ought in it. Nature has none.
  4. ArtifactAn artificial system, held together to its oughts and bounds by standing on a side, confidence built by practicing the interface.A side is where you stand. Outside, you see what the artifact faces. Inside, you see what is behind it. From either side you see the interface, never the other side. Stand on the edge and see both, and nothing has to cross; the artifact then collapses from disuse, not error.
  5. InterfaceThe component that transcribes: it carries something from one side to the other in a form the other side can use. Every crossing costs time, paid by whoever stands there.When you want something, all you give is wall time.
  6. PracticeThe interface has to pulse. Confidence is measured by practicing: bring the iron near and see whether it moves. Every crossing is a row, and the rows add up.

No confidence score is printed beside these six. Confidence is not stated; it is built, one crossing at a time, below.

The practice

Punch a card. Cross it. Spin the wheel.

Write the ask on a 2×4 card, as short as it can be said. Name what already exists around it and where you stand. Pick the least intervention. Then cross: do it, note the wall time, and check it against reality. Did the problem get resolved? If not, ask first what you said, gave or did. Every crossing punches a hole. Nothing is erased.

Oughts

Understand the nature of the problem. Work around it.

  1. Understand the nature of the problem, the human, and the systems involved.
  2. Work with what exists before introducing something new.
  3. Keep concerns orthogonal. Compose capabilities without recreating their internals.
  4. Make the desired action playable, rather than merely possible.
  5. Constrain dangerous operations structurally, rather than depending on perfect human judgment.
  6. Eliminate unnecessary human intervention, including repeated explanation.
  7. Test behavior in reality. An explanation is not a demonstration.
  8. Measure the outcome. Does the problem get resolved?
